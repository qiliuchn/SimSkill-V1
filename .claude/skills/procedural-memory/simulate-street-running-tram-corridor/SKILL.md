# Simulate Street-Running Tram Corridor

Models a tram/LRT/streetcar line sharing an urban arterial's right-of-way with general traffic — the in-street counterpart to `build-rail-corridor-with-railsignal`'s exclusive-right-of-way heavy rail. Nothing else in procedural memory covers a mode that (a) has its own vClass and infrastructure, (b) physically cannot steer around an obstruction, and (c) forces a real signal-phasing decision (a left turn crossing the reservation) rather than just another vehicle in mixed traffic. Reuses `design-bus-stop-placement-type-and-spacing`'s endogenous-dwell mechanism, `implement-transit-signal-priority`'s TSP controller (imported unchanged, retargeted to `vClass="tram"`), and `model-curbside-delivery-and-lane-blocking-externality`'s blockage mechanic — the genuinely new work is the reservation encoding, the tram-specific signal mechanics, and the break-even ridership methodology below.

## Tram vClass defaults — and why lane-changing must be structurally suppressed

SUMO's default `vClass="tram"` (probe with a bare vType, no other attributes) gives: **length 22.0 m, width 2.4 m, height 3.2 m, accel 1.0 m/s², decel 3.0 m/s², emergencyDecel 7.0 m/s², maxSpeed 22.22 m/s (80 km/h), minGap 2.5 m, guiShape "rail/railcar"**. Configure a realistic multi-car consist (e.g. 40 m) and tune accel/decel to the physical vehicle you're modeling — SUMO won't do this for you.

**The trap**: `rail-simulation-and-railsignal`'s `vClass="rail"` never lane-changes because it typically runs on single-lane exclusive track — there's nowhere to change lanes *to*. `vClass="tram"` on an ordinary multi-lane road lane has no such structural constraint, and a direct behavioral probe (2-lane edge, slow blocker in lane 0, tram departing in lane 0) shows it **does lane-change** by default, same as any other vehicle class. If the study requires a tram that stays in its lane (true of essentially any fixed-guideway or reserved-lane scenario), you must enforce it — SUMO's own defaults will not:

```python
# lane1_allow_for(arm): confine the tram structurally via lane permission,
# not vClass defaults. In this study's arm-B (exclusive) variant, lane1 is
# allow="tram" end-to-end -- there is no other legal lane for it to be in.
def lane1_allow_for(arm):
    if arm == "V0": return "passenger"
    if arm in ("C", "CP"): return "passenger tram"   # shared: mixed street running
    if arm in ("B", "BP"): return "tram"              # exclusive: median reservation
```

Verify the suppression held, don't assume it: scan `--lanechange-output` for the tram's vehicle id across every run/arm. A working confinement produces **zero** matches even under stress (e.g. a mid-block blockage the tram cannot route around — see "The tram trap" below); any non-zero count means the lane layout or permissions are leaking a second legal lane somewhere.

## The crossable-but-not-drivable reservation: two distinct permission mechanisms

A median/curbside tram reservation that cars may **cross at a junction** (typically a left turn) but may not **drive along** mid-block requires two separate SUMO mechanisms, not one:

1. **Lane permission** — `allow="tram"` on the reservation's running-length lane (e.g. `<lane index="1" allow="tram"/>` in the source `.edg.xml`, regenerated by `netconvert`). This alone blocks all car use, including the crossing movement, because the compiled net's `<connection>` elements carry **no `allow`/`disallow` of their own by default** — permission is inherited from the approach/via lane. So restricting only the *lane* would also illegally block a turn you want to keep legal.
2. **Connection permission** — an explicit `disallow="passenger"` added to the *specific* `<connection>` element for the movement you want to prohibit, post-editing the compiled `.net.xml` directly (there's no netconvert flag for "block this one turn"):

```python
def prohibit_arterial_left(net_path, out_path):
    # For every dir='l' connection FROM the general car lane (index 0) on an
    # arterial approach, add disallow="passenger" to that ONE <connection>.
    # The reservation lane's own permission is untouched -- this is a
    # separate, connection-level restriction on top of it.
    def sub_line(m):
        line = m.group(0)
        if (('from="AE_' in line or 'from="AW_' in line) and 'dir="l"' in line
                and 'fromLane="0"' in line and "disallow=" not in line):
            return line[:-2] + ' disallow="passenger"/>'
        return line
    ...
```

The crossing movement's own auto-generated internal junction lane (e.g. `:J3_18_0`) is a **separate SUMO lane object** from the mid-block reservation lane and does not automatically inherit the reservation's restriction — you must restrict the connection explicitly if you want the crossing prohibited, or leave it alone (inheriting the fromLane's permission) if you want it legal.

**Verify from three independent angles**, not just authoring intent:
- Grep the *compiled* net for the lane's `allow` and the connection's `disallow` — confirm both landed where you intended.
- Run `duarouter` **without** `--ignore-errors` on a trip desiring the prohibited movement. A returncode of 0 is *not* proof the movement is illegal — duarouter's job is "find *a* legal route," and it silently reroutes if one exists. Proof requires inspecting the **realized route's edge list** and confirming it detours (e.g. via a frontage/back street) rather than using the direct connection.
- Add a `laneData` detector on the reservation's mid-block lanes, filtered by `vTypes`, and confirm car `sampledSeconds ≈ 0` there while the turning movement's vehicles still complete their trips (i.e. the detour genuinely discharges the demand rather than gridlocking it).

## Signal control: ordinary `tlLogic`, not `railSignal` — and what adding the lane costs

Street-running is controlled by the road's ordinary `tlLogic`, **not** `railSignal` — `railSignal` exists specifically to arbitrate meets between opposing trains on a genuinely single-track section (see `[[rail-simulation-and-railsignal]]`), a conflict street-running doesn't have (each direction gets its own lane, signal-controlled like any other movement).

Two costs to measure, not assume:
- **Controlled-link count grows** as the tram lane is added — a real measured example on a 6-signal arterial: 14 links with no tram → 18 with a shared tram lane → 22 with an exclusive tram lane. Every `tlLogic` phase's state string must grow to match; a stale/mismatched string length is a common source of silent phase-mapping errors.
- **A protected left-turn phase measurably grows the cycle** wherever it's added — a real measured example: 66 s (4 phases, left turns simply prohibited) vs. 79 s (6 phases, dedicated protected-left phase) for the same junction. This isn't a local cost — see below.

Verify the tram's own link index actually maps to a green phase when it needs to proceed (`state[tram_link_idx] == 'G'` during the phase you intend it to use) — don't assume the classification you authored survived compilation unchanged.

## Resolving the left-turn-across-the-reservation conflict: prohibit + reroute beats a protected phase

Compare at least two treatments for the car left turn that crosses the reservation. A real measured comparison found **prohibiting the movement and rerouting it via a parallel street beats adding a protected left phase, corridor-wide** — by a wide margin (+25% to +66% total person-hours for "protected" vs. "prohibited" across two configurations tested). The mechanism: a protected phase's added cycle time (see above) taxes **every vehicle on every cycle** at that signal, not just the minority actually turning left — and it can even *increase* the tram's own signal wait despite existing to help it, because the tram now waits through a longer cycle too. The individual left-turner is faster under "protected" (direct movement vs. a detour), but that local win is swamped by the system-wide cost. Don't assume a protected phase is the safe/obvious choice for a reservation conflict — measure the corridor-wide tradeoff, because the individually-safer-feeling option can be the collectively worse one.

## The tram trap: a lane-confined vehicle has no escape from a non-evasive blockage

Inject a stopped/double-parked vehicle mid-block in the tram's lane (reuse `model-curbside-delivery-and-lane-blocking-externality`'s `parking="false"` `<stop>` mechanic — see `[[curbside-delivery-blocking-externality]]`). A bus in mixed traffic can change lanes around it; a tram confined to one lane by permission (see above) **cannot**, by construction.

**The effect lives in the tail and variance, not the mean** — check both, because the mean alone hides it. A real measured example: mean tram run time moved only +2–3% with the blockage in place, while **max run time jumped +35%, standard deviation +63%, and downstream headway coefficient-of-variation at the terminal stop +143%** in the exclusive-reservation arm. Counterintuitively, the arm with the more regular baseline service (exclusive reservation) can be **more fragile** to a single blockage than a noisier mixed-running baseline, in relative terms — because in the mixed-running arm some of that noise was already signal-cycle-driven variance the blockage adds to proportionally less, while the exclusive arm's tight baseline has more relative room to blow out. Report max/sd/headway-CV alongside the mean whenever evaluating a reservation's reliability, not just its average performance.

```python
# metrics.headway_cv_at_terminal: CV of the gap between successive tram stop
# ("ended") events at ONE terminal stop id -- pick the stop each tram
# reaches LAST in its direction, not the first (an easy off-by-one that
# silently produces a near-zero, meaningless CV).
def headway_cv_at_terminal(stops, terminal_stop_id):
    times = sorted(s["ended"] for s in stops if s["busStop"] == terminal_stop_id)
    gaps = [times[i+1] - times[i] for i in range(len(times) - 1)]
    m = statistics.mean(gaps); s = statistics.stdev(gaps)
    return dict(cv=(s / m if m else float("nan")), ...)
```

## Break-even ridership: when does the reservation pay for itself?

The decision-relevant question for a Class B (exclusive) vs. Class C (mixed) vs. no-tram right-of-way choice is not "which wins on average" but **"at what ridership does exclusivity's transit benefit repay its car-lane cost, and does that threshold move with congestion."**

1. Compute person-throughput and person-hours-of-delay per arm with **car occupancy as an explicit, varied parameter** (e.g. 1.2 and 1.5 persons/car), not a baked-in constant — applied to measured `car_total_veh_hours`, while transit throughput/hours come directly from measured `n_persons_completed_ride`/`person_total_hours` (see `[[managed-lanes-empty-lane-paradox-and-person-throughput]]` for the same person-throughput-over-vehicle-throughput discipline in a lane-conversion context).
2. Sweep tram ridership/load (e.g. via the boarding-rate parameter feeding the endogenous-dwell person demand) at **≥2 car-congestion levels**, and locate the crossing point(s) by linear interpolation between adjacent swept levels.
3. Expect the break-even point to be **congestion-dependent, not fixed** — a real measured example: an exclusive reservation never overtook mixed running on total person-hours at an uncongested car-demand level (tested up to ~880 riders/hr corridor-wide), but overtook it at ≈500–600 riders/hr once car demand was oversaturated — and never overtook the no-tram baseline at all within the tested range at either congestion level. The lane's opportunity cost only gets paid back once the car congestion it relieves is severe enough; report both "vs. mixed running" and "vs. no tram" break-evens separately, since they can differ enormously, and state explicitly that a break-even curve should not be extrapolated past its tested ridership range.

## Gotchas

- **An unsorted person route file silently drops persons, not just reorders them.** SUMO requires `<person>` elements in departure-time order and silently *ignores* (drops) any that appear out of order — a real run with an unsorted file lost 14 of 21 persons with no fatal error, just an easy-to-miss "ignoring 'pX'" warning. Sort by `depart=` before writing, exactly as you would for `<trip>`/`<vehicle>` elements.
- **`<laneData lanes="...">` does not filter what SUMO writes to the output file.** It dumps every lane in the network regardless of the `lanes=` attribute (verified directly) — filter by lane id when *reading* the output, not by trusting the attribute to have limited it at write time. A naive "sum everything in the file" reader will produce a wildly wrong aggregate.
- **A lane-permission classifier must be scoped to the arm it actually applies to.** Tagging a shared lane's car movements as "tram-only" in an arm where the lane is genuinely shared (rather than exclusive) mislabels a real, legal car movement as spurious-and-permanently-red — this produces actual gridlock and teleports, not just a cosmetic mislabeling, and is easy to miss if only the exclusive-reservation arm is spot-checked.
- **netconvert auto-generates connections a real route never uses** (e.g. a tram-lane-sourced right or left turn nobody routes through) purely because the lane geometry permits it. Giving these unused connections a protected `'G'` alongside a real car movement that lands on the same single-lane destination trips SUMO's "unsafe green phase... targeted by 2 G-links" check. Fix by leaving genuinely-unused connections red, and by making right turns (and any permissive left) `'g'` rather than `'G'` wherever two legal sources can feed the same single-lane destination.
- **A special-case signal/lane treatment (e.g. the left-turn prohibition above) must not leak into arms that don't need it** — applying B/BP-specific left-turn control logic to V0/C/CP as well will permanently red-light a movement those arms never intended to restrict, gridlocking demand that should flow freely.
