# Model Grade-Aware Heavy-Vehicle Performance and Climbing Lanes

Repairs a documented SUMO limitation — `[[heavy-vehicle-passenger-car-equivalent-in-sumo]]` and `[[road-gradient-and-energy-consumption]]` establish that road grade feeds SUMO's emission/energy models but reaches **no** built-in car-following model, so trucks climb a 6% grade at free-flow speed by default — and uses the repair to answer a real geometric-design question: when does a climbing lane actually pay for itself. This is the first skill in memory whose core deliverable is a vehicle-dynamics model rather than a controller, geometry, or analysis pipeline.

## Re-confirm the gap quantitatively before building anything

Don't assume the documented null result generalizes to your SUMO version or your specific car-following model — measure full speed-distance profiles (not just point checks) across multiple car-following models (Krauss/IDM/EIDM/ACC/CACC/W99 is a representative spread) and multiple grades. Verified: **0.0% deviation at every checkpoint for every model** at every tested grade — the null result is total, not partial, across every stock road car-following model.

**Test native vType/device levers before writing a custom controller — most don't reach longitudinal dynamics, but one does.** Verified not to change trajectory at all: `mass` (a 10x mass change produced an identical trajectory), `emissionClass`, and battery-device power/drag parameters (`maximumPower`, `airDragCoefficient`, `rollDragCoefficient`, `constantPowerIntake` — these feed only energy bookkeeping). Verified to produce only a negligible artifact, not a real effect: EIDM tuning attributes gave a ~0.06% residual traceable to 3D lane-length elongation on a graded edge (Pythagorean geometry, not dynamics). **The one native lever that genuinely responds to grade**: `carFollowModel="Rail"` with a `trainType` on `vClass="rail"` track — verified to collapse a vehicle's max speed toward zero as grade increases (a real rail-adhesion response) — but this comes with disqualifying constraints for a highway climbing-lane study (see below).

## Enumerate injection mechanisms and choose on measured grounds, not convenience

At least three mechanisms are plausible; verify each rather than picking the first one that compiles:

1. **A TraCI per-step controller applying a physics-derived speed *ceiling*** (chosen approach) — each step, compute attainable acceleration from tractive effort (rated power / current speed, capped by a traction-limit force), minus grade resistance (`mg·sinθ`), rolling resistance (`mg·Cr·cosθ`), and aerodynamic drag (`½ρCdAv²`), then apply the result via `traci.vehicle.setMaxSpeed()` **only** — never `setSpeed()`. This is the critical safety-preserving design choice: constraining *capability* (what speed is achievable) while leaving SUMO's own car-following model fully in charge of *safety* (what speed is actually taken, given the vehicle ahead). Use `v_base = min(v_attain_prev, v_actual)` when integrating the next step's acceleration, so the physics ceiling never "runs ahead" of a truck that's currently slower than its own physics would allow (e.g. because it's stuck behind slower traffic) — otherwise the controller would let the truck suddenly accelerate away the moment its physics ceiling exceeds its current (traffic-constrained) speed, which is physically wrong.
2. **Repurposing `carFollowModel="Rail"`** — genuinely grade-responsive (see above), but verified disqualifying in practice: with realistic freight parameters it stalls completely above a modest grade (not a usable crawl-speed curve), requires dedicated `vClass="rail"` track structurally incompatible with mixed highway traffic, and has essentially no lane-changing behavior on single-track topology (see `[[rail-simulation-and-railsignal]]`) — unusable for a multi-lane highway climbing-lane study specifically, though it may suit a genuinely rail-adjacent grade problem.
3. **Static per-edge vClass speed limits** as a cheap approximation — verified to produce an unrealistic abrupt speed drop (not the gradual AASHTO decay curve) and, being a single network-wide number, cannot differentiate trucks by their own weight-to-power ratio (a low-power truck and a high-power truck get the identical authored speed). Reject for anything beyond a very rough sketch.

## Validate the physics against an independent implementation — not against itself

Write the cross-validation as a genuinely separate program (verify it imports nothing from your controller module) that independently integrates the same equations of motion — e.g. RK4 plus a bisection solver for the terminal crawl speed. Compare the TraCI-realized crawl speed against this independent analytical prediction across your parameter range (grade, weight-to-power ratio). Verified: **0.00–0.67% relative error at every grade ≥2%** across three tested weight-to-power ratios — near-exact agreement. At grade≈0%, expect *larger* apparent disagreement (verified up to ~20%) that is not actually a bug: the ungoverned analytic crawl speed can exceed the road's own posted speed limit or the vType's `maxSpeed`, so a *pre-existing* SUMO speed ceiling binds before your physics ceiling does — check which ceiling is actually active before treating a grade≈0% mismatch as a validation failure.

**Reproduce the qualitative AASHTO truck-performance-curve shape**, not just a single terminal speed: start the truck at highway cruise speed (not from rest) entering the grade, and verify crawl speed is approached gradually (verified: within 5% of the analytic terminal value after roughly 1–4 km, depending on grade and weight-to-power ratio) and monotonically decreases with steeper grade and with a worse (higher) weight-to-power ratio.

## Prove car-following safety is preserved — this is not optional

A controller that constrains only `setMaxSpeed()` should still be checked, not assumed safe:
- **Zero collisions, zero teleports** on a genuine mixed car/truck test scenario on the graded facility.
- **The ceiling must be provably non-binding when the truck is already traffic-constrained** — construct a test where a truck is queued behind a slower lead vehicle and verify the physics ceiling stays at or above the truck's actual (car-following-limited) speed at essentially every simulation step. If the ceiling ever drops below the actual speed while the truck is following, the controller is overriding car-following behavior rather than merely capping unconstrained capability, which defeats the entire safety-preservation design goal.
- **Report the runtime cost honestly.** A per-step TraCI controller is expensive — verified roughly 10-45x slower wall-clock than the equivalent grade-blind subprocess run, with tens of thousands of TraCI calls per run. This is a real, material cost to disclose alongside the model's benefits, not a footnote.

## Build the climbing-lane facility and verify geometry from the compiled net

Reuse `model-road-gradient-effects-on-energy`'s discipline: verify realized grade from the **compiled** net's lane-shape z-data, never from the source node/edge XML — netconvert can adjust edge lengths at junctions in ways that shift the effective grade if you only check the source. Reuse `evaluate-two-lane-highway-with-hcm-and-passing-lanes`'s added-lane-with-tapers-and-lane-drop template for the climbing-lane geometry itself, and `model-vclass-lane-permissions` for a truck/slow-vehicle-restricted lane variant (`disallow` on the general-purpose lanes over the climbing segment).

**A general-purpose (unrestricted) auxiliary lane over a grade functions as a car-passing lane, not a truck lane, unless mandated.** Verified: trucks, being power-limited rather than congestion-limited, have essentially no independent incentive to move into an added lane — measured truck use of a GP added lane ran from near-zero up to the low teens percent depending on conditions — while cars flocked to it (roughly half of car traffic, by cell) specifically to pass slow trucks stuck in the base lanes. A truck-restricted variant achieves ~100% truck usage of the added lane by construction (deterministic, not something to re-verify per run) but forces the mode-share question explicitly rather than letting it emerge. Don't assume "add a lane near trucks" produces "trucks use the lane" — measure it.

## Design findings: when a climbing lane actually pays

Verified on one facility/demand configuration (report your own facility's numbers; treat these as illustrative magnitudes, not universal constants):

- **Grade-blindness doesn't just under-predict a number — it can flip the actual design decision.** A grade-aware analysis found a climbing lane's car-delay benefit at a severe grade/length combination roughly an order of magnitude larger than anything the grade-blind model could show anywhere in the same parameter sweep. A naive "statistically significant benefit exists" check can trigger true under both models (a lane relieves some baseline friction regardless of grade), which is the wrong test — compare the *magnitude* a delay-based warrant would actually act on, not just significance.
- **The AASHTO speed-reduction criterion (a truck losing ~15 km/h/10 mph) and the delay-based economic warrant are not the same threshold, and can disagree substantially.** Under realized mixed-traffic conditions (not clean free-flow), the speed-reduction criterion can trigger far more permissively — even at short/mild grades — than the point at which a climbing lane actually produces a statistically distinguishable delay benefit, which may require both a longer/steeper grade *and* a high enough truck share. Don't treat the speed-reduction check alone as a reliable proxy for whether a lane will actually pay off in delay terms; check both.
- **Restricting the added lane to trucks vs. leaving it general-purpose has a genuine truck-share-dependent crossover** — at low truck share, restriction can measurably hurt overall car delay (dedicating capacity to a traffic-light minority); at high truck share, restriction can measurably help. Locate this crossover empirically for your own demand mix rather than assuming either policy dominates universally.
- **Grade-aware physics changes the story asymmetrically between uphill and downhill.** A genuine capacity-loss mechanism (measurably lower served flow under grade-aware behavior vs. grade-blind) can be present uphill and genuinely absent downhill on the same facility, even though grade-aware trucks are somewhat slower than grade-blind trucks in *both* directions — the uphill effect is a real capacity mechanism, not just a generic "grade makes trucks slower" statement, and shouldn't be assumed to have an equal-and-opposite downhill counterpart without checking (variance/speed-differential effects downhill do not automatically mirror the uphill capacity mechanism — verify each direction's actual governing mechanism separately rather than assuming symmetry).

## Gotchas

- **`traci.vehicle.getSlope()` returns degrees, not percent** — verify this empirically before wiring any physics off of it; a silent percent/degree mix-up would badly distort every force calculation.
- **A per-vehicle `speedFactor="normc(...)"` clones a synthetic vType named `"<original>@<vehicleID>"`** — an exact-match filter on the original vType name will silently match nothing once this is in play; use a prefix/`startswith` match instead.
- **Give slow vehicles a genuine tail buffer beyond your nominal simulation end time**, or they'll still be in-network at cutoff and get silently excluded from completion-based statistics as "unfinished" — this can make a crawling truck's own data vanish from exactly the analysis that's supposed to characterize it.
- **`laneData` output nests `<lane>` elements under `<edge>` within `<interval>`** (unlike `edgeData`, where `<edge>` is a direct child of `<interval>`) — a naive `interval.findall("lane")` silently returns nothing; use `.//lane`.
- **A hardcoded exit-edge id for measuring served/exiting flow breaks the moment you mirror the network for the opposite direction** (e.g. an uphill "departure" edge id won't exist on the downhill mirror, which exits somewhere else) — parameterize the exit edge per direction, and if you discover this after the fact, a post-hoc re-parse of already-saved edgeData XML (no re-simulation needed) is often sufficient to recover the missing metric.
